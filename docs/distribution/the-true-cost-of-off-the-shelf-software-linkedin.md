Most businesses calculate their software spend by adding up the monthly invoices. That number is wrong by 30 to 50 percent.

The licensing fees are only part of what a subscription stack costs. A 10-person team paying $9,600/year in licensing is not paying $9,600/year for software. The rest includes the cost of connecting tools that do not share data natively. It also includes time spent working around features baked into the plan tier that nobody opens.

The integration layer alone typically adds $4,000 to $18,000/year above the licensing number for a 15-person organization connecting five tools. That figure includes middleware subscriptions running $100 to $300/month plus 2 to 5 hours of developer time per month when API changes break automations.

Per-seat pricing amplifies the problem as teams scale. A team growing from 10 to 25 people sees its licensing costs 2.5x before adding a single new tool. Most plan tiers are structured so the only path to more seats is a tier with more features, meaning teams pay for functionality nobody will open.

Key numbers from the piece:

- A 10-person stack at $800/month in licensing typically runs $1,100 to $1,400/month all-in when integration and maintenance costs are included
- Most teams activate 30 to 50 percent of the features in their current plan tier
- A 15-person team losing 45 minutes/day per person to context switching loses the equivalent of $59,000/year in productive labor
- A CRM migration typically costs $2,000 to $4,500 in internal labor, a number most businesses overestimate and use to justify staying subscribed too long
- Custom software becomes cost-competitive with a mature subscription stack at year two to year four, and saves approximately $41,000 over three years at 25 seats

The costs are designed to be invisible. Integration overhead lands in a developer's timesheet. Switching costs never appear as a line item at all.

The audit takes about an hour and starts with 12 months of credit card statements.
