Most operational spreadsheets are built by one person and inherited by five. At some point, two people edit different copies of the same file, and by Friday three versions exist with no clear way to determine which one is authoritative.

That is the first of four specific failure patterns. The second is formula opacity: business-critical logic lives in a calculation one person wrote two years ago that no one else on the team fully understands. The third is reconciliation overhead: someone now spends recurring hours comparing spreadsheets or copying data between files, work that is entirely error-prone and invisible on any org chart. The fourth is the missing query: the spreadsheet tracks what exists but cannot generate a report, flag an anomaly, or send an alert without a manual audit first.

Most businesses reach this point when two or more of these are present at the same time. Adding more tabs or formulas treats the symptoms. A spreadsheet is a file, not a system.

When the decision to replace has been made, two paths:

- SaaS tools cost $15 to $100 per user per month at the accessible tier; a five-person team pays $75 to $1,500 per month
- Custom software runs $15,000 to $60,000 for a focused internal tool, delivered in 8 to 16 weeks
- The annual opportunity cost of managing and reconciling the existing spreadsheet: $3,640 to $31,200 in staff time, before errors
- Migration from spreadsheet to replacement takes 4 to 12 weeks; data cleaning alone takes 1 to 3 weeks
- If SaaS would cost $2,000 or more per month for your team size, custom software is worth getting a quote on

The most common mistake is picking a replacement before documenting what the current spreadsheet actually does. The apparent function is always simpler than the actual function. Choosing a tool first, then discovering it cannot handle the real requirements, leads to significant workarounds or a second migration within a year.
